## Fig. 1. Framework element 1: A pre-specified perturbation-to-dependency bridge defines the truth object in HCC38 and HCC1143

**a,** Study workflow and frozen recovery object. The framework defines a phenotype-aligned truth object by aligning absolute mean perturbation shift (transcriptomic truth) to CRISPR DepMap dependency (fitness endpoint) before any model-scoring step. The object is unpacked into three pre-specified components: the truth signal (absolute mean perturbation shift), the alignment endpoint (CRISPR dependency), and the category rule (pre-specified 25/75 joint percentile grid). The adjudicated entrant set includes the shared-mean baseline, GEARS, foundation-model entrants, linear controls, null references, and rebuttal checks. Truth object definition constitutes Framework Element 1 and is frozen before model comparison. **b,** Pre-specified 25/75 category rule. Targets were classified on a pre-specified joint grid using the 25th and 75th percentiles of transcriptomic perturbation shift and CRISPR dependency, with corner-defined categories and a retained middle band. **c,** HCC38 target-level joint grid. Axes show within-context rank percentiles (`shift_quantile` and `depmap_quantile`), with n, aligned Spearman rho, and Q1 anchor count annotated in the panel. **d,** HCC1143 target-level joint grid. Axes show within-context rank percentiles, with n, aligned Spearman rho, and Q1 anchor count annotated in the panel. Spearman rho is computed from the raw absolute values (`real_shift_mean_abs`, `depmap_gene_dependency`); the quantile display standardizes the two axes to a common [0,1] scale and directly shows the 25/75 percentile cutoffs used for category assignment. **e,** Grid composition across the two primary contexts. Q2 transcriptomic-excess and Q3 dependency-excess targets were not observed in either primary context and are retained as zero-count categories in the composition summary. **f,** Bridge strength summary. Visual elements are: (i) point, the observed aligned Spearman rho between absolute mean perturbation shift and CRISPR DepMap dependency on target-level pairs, with n = 47 (HCC38) and n = 48 (HCC1143); (ii) vertical error bar, the Fisher z-transform 95% confidence interval of the point estimate, computed in closed form from n and the observed rho and describing sampling uncertainty of the point itself, not a bootstrap; (iii) gray band, the 95% envelope of an empirical permutation null obtained by shuffling the target-to-DepMap pairing within each context 1000 times under a fixed seed, describing the range of aligned Spearman rho expected under the rho = 0 null hypothesis of no target-level alignment. The point estimate, the point-level CI, and the null envelope are distinct quantities and are plotted together only to show that the observed bridge strength lies well outside the permutation null envelope in both primary contexts (empirical two-sided p = 0.001 for each context), consistent with a structured perturbation-fitness bridge rather than random association. The bridge is interpreted as a structured target-level recovery object with pre-specified categories and is structurally above the permutation null; it is not interpreted as fully deconfounded causal proof.

## Fig. 2. Framework element 2: Covariate-aware anchor tiering separates recurrent bridge support from target proof

The panels are arranged as an evidence-first narrative: panels a-d show that the four final stable anchors (PFDN5, PRPF6, PMF1, ZNF131) are indistinguishable at the structural axes (shift/dependency quantile, recurrence, stability fraction); panel e then presents the per-anchor covariate TVD evidence that separates PFDN5 from the other three; panel f reads out the resulting claim tiers. No asterisks or covariate colour-codings are used in panels a-d, so that the "four indistinguishable stable candidates" framing is not pre-judged before the TVD adjudication. Covariate-exposed cells are marked only in panel e (darker ochre fill with bold numerical value). Tier colours in panels c and f follow one convention: green denotes primary but qualified (stable anchors in panel c; PFDN5 in panel f), darker ochre denotes supporting only (panel f), and lighter sand denotes cutoff-sensitive supporting objects (panel c).

**a,** Shared-canonical anchor ranking. Paired shift (open) and dependency (filled) within-cell-line quantile means for the seven shared-canonical candidates, ordered by the mean within-cell-line joint quantile (mean of shift and dependency quantile means). The grey band behind each row spans the cutoff-sensitivity range of the shift and dependency quantiles. PFDN5 is highlighted as the primary-but-qualified headline anchor. **b,** Low-ink recurrence matrix across the two primary HCC contexts (HCC1143 and HCC38) for the four final stable anchors. Each cell reports the within-cell-line mean joint quantile (mean of shift and dependency quantiles); recurrence is inferred from consistently high values across both contexts, and green cell borders mark Q1-anchor status. The PFDN5 row is highlighted. **c,** Stability fraction across stable and cutoff-sensitive shared-canonical objects. This panel screens only at the structural-stability axis: all four stable anchors (PFDN5, PRPF6, PMF1, ZNF131) share the same green bar and are not distinguishable here; the five cutoff-sensitive supporting objects (ENY2, NPM1, RPS3, RUVBL2, ZBTB17) use the lighter sand tone. Horizontal whiskers indicate the cutoff-range envelope. **d,** Final stable anchors retain high shift and dependency ranks across HCC38 and HCC1143, with numeric quantile means annotated per bar and the PFDN5 column highlighted; no covariate-level marking is drawn at this structural layer. **e,** Per-anchor covariate Total Variation Distance (TVD) matrix across five covariate axes (barcode gem group, UMI-threshold bin, UMI-quantile bin, detected-genes quantile bin, total-signal quantile bin) and the two HCC contexts, for the four final stable anchors. Cells with TVD > 0.25 (the pre-specified hard imbalance threshold, corresponding to a quarter of the probability mass being displaced between target and control distributions) are filled in darker ochre and bold-typed; cells at or below 0.25 are rendered in neutral gray. PFDN5 (row highlighted in pale green) has TVD <= 0.25 across all 10 audited cells, whereas PMF1, PRPF6, and ZNF131 each exceed 0.25 on UMI-related axes. This is the panel where the four stable anchors are first actually separable, and it provides the direct evidence underlying panel f. **f,** Compact anchor claim matrix (conclusion). PFDN5 is the only primary-but-qualified anchor and is covariate-clean; PMF1, PRPF6, and ZNF131 are retained as supporting only because panel e shows they are covariate-exposed despite being structurally stable. This tiering implements Framework Element 2: structural recurrence, cutoff stability, and covariate governance are distinct evidence dimensions, and target-level wording is capped below fully deconfounded causal claims. Shared anchors support the existence of a structured perturbation-fitness bridge but are not fully deconfounded target-level causal proof.

## Fig. 3. Framework element 3: Architecture-aware model adjudication reveals an asymmetric recovery pattern

After the truth object and anchor tiers were frozen, model recovery was adjudicated across three pre-specified architecture-aware dimensions: canonical backbone recovery, shift-excess identification, and structure-versus-context separation. Across HCC38 and HCC1143, formal entrants do not outperform the shared-mean baseline on backbone recovery, yet they occupy a reproducible asymmetric recovery pattern between backbone recovery and structure-versus-context separation. The term "asymmetric recovery pattern" is used deliberately rather than "trade-off" because the present evidence demonstrates differential recovery across dimensions without establishing that the dimensions are mutually constraining within a single model. All three adjudication metrics are retained side by side because no single metric is sufficient to summarize model performance under the frozen architecture-aware adjudication; the bounded wording is stated in the final sentence of this caption rather than being embedded as an axis-level claim.

**a,** Three adjudication metrics separate recovery modes across entrants. Absolute scores for backbone recovery, shift-excess identification, and structure-versus-context separation are shown for the shared-mean baseline, the formal GEARS recipe, foundation-model entrants (Geneformer, scGPT), linear controls (LM low-rank, LM + Geneformer G, LM + scGPT G), and the null reference. The shared-mean baseline leads on backbone recovery (0.807 versus 0.660 for GEARS), whereas the formal GEARS recipe leads on structure-versus-context separation (0.428 versus 0.353). Shift-excess identification is tied at 0.333 for both formal entrants. Under the pre-specified definition (pairwise superiority probability with ties counted as 0.5, chance expectation = 0.5; Methods), this value falls below the chance level, indicating that neither entrant has recovered the shift-excess component beyond the background expectation under the current architecture definition. The null row is retained only as a visually anchored reference band. **b,** Baseline leads backbone recovery, whereas GEARS leads context separation. A two-metric paired-dot summary contrasts only the shared-mean baseline and the pre-specified formal GEARS recipe: the baseline scores higher on backbone recovery (0.807 versus 0.660), whereas GEARS scores higher on structure-versus-context separation (0.428 versus 0.353). Shift-excess identification is tied between the two formal entrants and is therefore summarized in panel a rather than duplicated here. **c,** Entrants occupy an asymmetric recovery space. Backbone recovery versus structure-versus-context separation is plotted for the shared-mean baseline, the formal GEARS recipe, foundation-model entrants, and the linear controls. The shared-mean baseline and formal GEARS recipe are highlighted as the reference anchor and best entrant, respectively; Geneformer and scGPT are labelled as supporting foundation entrants, and the linear controls remain below the two headline entrants in backbone recovery. A lightly shaded upper-right region is shown only as an illustrative visual aid: no entrant occupies this region, with the shared-mean baseline sitting below its lower boundary and the formal GEARS recipe sitting to the left of its left boundary, consistent with an asymmetric recovery pattern rather than a uniformly dominant recipe. This shaded region is not a decision threshold and is not used for scoring or adjudication; no frontier or iso-total reference line is drawn because the present evidence supports an architecture-level diagnosis rather than a characterized frontier. Together, these analyses support a bounded model-side interpretation: the shared-mean baseline remains the backbone primary reference, whereas GEARS is retained as an architecture-level diagnosis rather than an overall HCC38/HCC1143 primary winner. Neither entrant recovers the full frozen architecture.

## Fig. 4. Framework element 4: Finite-budget rebuttal tests with a pre-specified stop rule do not close the backbone recovery gap

**a,** Analysis-locked finite-budget GEARS candidates. Six pre-specified recipes were evaluated under an unchanged truth object and scoring system: the formal GEARS recipe and five one-parameter local variants varying epochs, learning rate, or weight decay. This panel defines the local rebuttal neighborhood rather than an open-ended optimization procedure. **b,** Pre-specified rebuttal recovery space. The shared-mean baseline, GEARS formal, the five GEARS sweep candidates, and three embedding-based linear controls are placed in the same backbone-recovery versus structure/context-separation space. Diamond markers denote the three linear controls: scGPT-ridge, Geneformer-ridge, and the low-rank decoder. Under the pre-specified stop rule—GEARS would not be promoted to the HCC38/HCC1143 primary winner unless a finite-budget candidate closed the backbone-recovery gap to the shared-mean baseline—no tested candidate crosses the baseline backbone boundary; the best sweep candidate (backbone recovery = 0.643) still falls short of the shared-mean baseline by 0.164 backbone-recovery units. **c,** Per-context residual backbone gap. GEARS formal, the best GEARS sweep candidate, and the three linear controls are plotted as backbone-recovery differences relative to the shared-mean baseline in HCC38 and HCC1143. All tested candidates remain below the baseline in both primary contexts, indicating that the finite-budget GEARS sweep and the embedding-based linear controls do not close the backbone gap. GEARS training is not rerun during figure production, and persistence of the gap should not be read as evidence for a hidden recipe winner or complete model-side closure. The pre-specified stop rule (Framework Element 4) ensures that GEARS is retained as an architecture-level diagnosis rather than promoted as the primary winner.

## Fig. 5. Framework elements 6 and 7: Temporal architecture, endpoint hierarchy, and covariate governance define the benchmark claim space

**a,** Covariate boundary in HCC38 and HCC1143 (Framework element 7). Mean target-control Total Variation Distance (TVD) is shown on a shared color scale for five covariate stratifications (barcode gem group, detected-gene bin, UMI signal, UMI threshold, UMI quantile). Brown scatter glyphs encode the count of candidate targets with TVD > 0.25; a light gray "0" marks no crossing target. The gold bracket visually groups the three UMI-related rows. Residual imbalance concentrates on UMI-related axes, motivating covariate-aware rather than fully deconfounded target-level wording. **b,** Endpoint hierarchy across all four bridge contexts (Framework element 6). For HCC38, HCC1143, K562 7d, and K562 13d, each row shows aligned Spearman correlation for DepMap CRISPR gene dependency (green) and RNAi DEMETER2 (gray), joined by a light segment; platform-specific shared-target ranges (n) are annotated. The n labels report the smaller and larger platform-specific target counts after endpoint joining; differences arise from endpoint availability rather than from changing the plotted bridge object (SMAD5 lacks the CRISPR endpoint in HCC38; SMAD5 and ZNF131 are platform-specific endpoint dropouts across the HCC1143 comparison; GABPA lacks the RNAi endpoint in K562). Aligned Spearman is higher for CRISPR than for RNAi in every context without exception: 0.726 versus 0.276 (HCC38), 0.779 versus 0.384 (HCC1143), 0.733 versus 0.333 (K562 7d), and 0.515 versus 0.300 (K562 13d). This consistent directionality across four perturbation contexts spanning two cell lineages (breast, leukemia), two datasets, and two perturbation platforms establishes a clear endpoint hierarchy: CRISPR DepMap is the primary bridge readout; RNAi DEMETER2 provides a cross-platform sensitivity endpoint but cannot replace the primary readout. **c,** K562 temporal stratification: stronger rank alignment at 7d despite smaller perturbation magnitude (Framework element 6). Paired boxplots summarize per-target mean absolute real shift at 7d and 13d; thin lines link paired targets; jittered points show the per-target values. The headline shows a two-sided paired Wilcoxon test of 7d versus 13d. Shift magnitude is significantly larger at 13d, yet the aligned Spearman rank bridge with CRISPR DepMap is stronger at 7d (0.733) than at 13d (0.515), as shown in panel b. This dissociation—larger perturbation magnitude at the later time point co-occurring with weaker dependency-aligned rank structure—indicates temporal stratification rather than monotonic improvement: early perturbation responses may be more tightly coupled to fitness-relevant architecture, while later time points accumulate perturbation signal that is accompanied by weaker dependency-aligned rank organization. The K562 layer is retained as supplementary temporal evidence under A0/A1/B tiering (see Methods), not as a content-replication co-pillar. Together, these audits define the primary (CRISPR DepMap), sensitivity (RNAi DEMETER2), and supplementary (K562 temporal) evidence boundaries of the benchmark. Framework elements 6 and 7 operate jointly: temporal architecture and endpoint hierarchy (element 6) establish the evidence structure across contexts and readouts, while covariate governance (element 7) caps claim language at the level supported by the available metadata resolution.

## Extended Data Fig. 2. Robustness of perturbation-fitness bridge metric selection

Candidate perturbation-shift summaries derived from the expression response vector, including full-transcriptome mean absolute shift, L2 magnitude, DEG-burden, and top-k shift summaries, were evaluated against matched DepMap CRISPR dependency and RNAi sensitivity endpoints. These analyses are robustness checks rather than post hoc optimization; the primary bridge metric remains full-transcriptome mean absolute shift versus CRISPR dependency. **a,** Top-n gene-subset sensitivity. Genes were ranked either by control-condition expression or by perturbation-response magnitude, and aligned Spearman correlation with DepMap CRISPR dependency was recomputed using the top 100, 500, 1000, 2000, or all genes for mean absolute shift, L2 magnitude, and DEG-burden summaries. Curves show the mean across HCC38 and HCC1143, with the shaded band spanning the two primary contexts. **b,** Metric and CRISPR endpoint robustness. The heat map shows mean aligned Spearman correlation across HCC38 and HCC1143 for candidate truth metrics crossed with the two CRISPR DepMap readouts; the boxed cell marks the retained primary metric and endpoint. **c,** Endpoint sensitivity. The endpoint-gap summary compares CRISPR dependency with DEMETER2 RNAi dependency for mean absolute shift and L2 magnitude across the two primary HCC contexts and the K562 7d/13d supplementary temporal contexts, supporting CRISPR dependency as the primary endpoint and RNAi as a weaker sensitivity readout. **d,** Control-subsampling robustness. Aligned Spearman correlation with DepMap CRISPR dependency was recomputed across repeated control-cell subsamples for candidate truth metrics in HCC38 and HCC1143; points show the mean and horizontal intervals show the 2.5th-97.5th percentile range across subsamples. **e,** Whole-transcriptome shift versus target-gene self-expression in driving the fitness bridge. Each point represents one perturbation target in HCC38 (left sub-panel) or HCC1143 (right sub-panel). The x-axis shows the target gene's own log2 fold-change; the y-axis shows DepMap CRISPR dependency. Point color and size encode the whole-transcriptome mean absolute shift. Spearman correlation between target-gene log2FC and dependency is negative (rho = -0.21, HCC38; rho = -0.35, HCC1143), whereas whole-transcriptome shift shows strong positive alignment (rho = 0.73, HCC38; rho = 0.77, HCC1143; see Fig. 1f). Thus, the truth-DepMap bridge is carried by genome-wide transcriptional displacement, not by the magnitude of target-gene depletion.

## Extended Data Fig. 3. K562 temporal evidence and large-scale bridge confirmation

**a,** K562 temporal bridge-magnitude dissociation (GSE90063). Aligned Spearman correlation with CRISPR DepMap dependency and mean perturbation-shift magnitude are shown for the 7d temporal-sensitivity panel and the 13d formal supplementary bridge test. Across both summaries, 13d has larger perturbation magnitude, whereas the rank bridge is weaker than at 7d (aligned Spearman rho = 0.733 at 7d versus 0.515 at 13d). This dissociation supports temporal stratification rather than monotonic strengthening. **b,** Replogle K562 essential day 7 large-scale perturbation-fitness bridge. Scatter of `real_shift_mean_abs` versus `depmap_gene_dependency` for 1,882 matched CRISPRi targets, displayed as within-context rank percentiles (0–1 axes, matching the convention of Fig. 1c,d). Dashed lines mark the 25th and 75th percentiles. Quadrant coloring follows Fig. 1: Q1 anchor (red, n = 202), Q2 transcriptomic-excess (green, n = 34), Q3 dependency-excess (blue, n = 57), Q4 low-information (gray, n = 198), retained middle band (light gray, n = 1,391). ## Extended Data Fig. 5. Exploratory pathway-response polarity heatmap

Pathway enrichment was used as an exploratory response-level summary under Framework Element 5 and was not used to define the benchmark truth object, endpoint hierarchy, or model-adjudication criteria. **a,** Pathway normalized enrichment score (NES) heat map across the pre-specified display targets in HCC38, HCC1143, K562 7d, and K562 13d. Rows show selected benchmark anchors and high-variance response examples; columns show the pre-specified Hallmark response panel. Asterisks indicate pathway enrichments passing FDR < 0.10. Right-hand summaries report same-target partner-context pathway Spearman correlation and sign-agreement fraction across all Hallmark pathways. A biologically noteworthy dissociation is visible: PFDN5 and PRPF6 maintain strong fitness-dependency linkage across HCC38 and HCC1143 (Fig. 2), yet their Hallmark pathway-level response patterns show low sign agreement across the two cell lines. This observation, based on two anchor genes (PFDN5, PRPF6) and two cell-line contexts (HCC38, HCC1143), suggests that fitness anchoring (dependency linkage) and transcriptional response wiring (pathway execution) can be partially dissociable properties, an observation aligned with the framework's methodological separation of expression reconstruction and phenotype relevance as independent dimensions requiring separate adjudication. Whether this dissociation generalizes to larger target panels or additional lineages remains an open question. Pathway-level results are treated as framework-generated exploratory context rather than as a portable pathway mechanism.

## Extended Data Fig. 6. Robustness audits for bridge structure, model adjudication and endpoint hierarchy

Four complementary robustness audits address potential reviewer concerns about baseline interpretation, anchor influence, small-sample stability and endpoint hierarchy. **a,** Shared-mean baseline non-oracle audit. Forest-style dot plot compares the original shared-mean baseline backbone recovery scores with leave-one-target-out (LOTO) mean [min, max] and cross-context baseline scores (HCC38 canonical backbone evaluated in HCC1143, and vice versa). The original backbone recovery of 0.807 is similar to the LOTO mean of 0.791, and cross-context scores remain 0.77–0.84, well above the null expectation of 0.5. This supports that the shared-mean baseline advantage reflects recurrent canonical-backbone architecture rather than target self-information or oracle access. **b,** Leave-anchor-out bridge robustness. Paired dumbbell plot comparing aligned Spearman rho for the full target set versus after removing all four stable anchors (PFDN5, PRPF6, PMF1, ZNF131) in HCC38 and HCC1143. HCC38 rho decreases from 0.726 to 0.665, and HCC1143 rho from 0.779 to 0.725, with jackknife worst-case drops of 0.015 or less. Bridge remains materially positive and is a target-level structure rather than a single-anchor artifact. **c,** Bootstrap target-resampling uncertainty for model adjudication scores. Bar-and-marker display of the bootstrap Δ (baseline − GEARS) across three metrics with 95% confidence intervals (1,000 target resamples). Backbone recovery Δ = +0.147 [0.087, 0.197], 100% sign stability (baseline leads); shift-excess identification Δ ≈ 0, 39% sign stability (unstable); structure-versus-context separation Δ = −0.075 [−0.123, −0.025], 99.9% sign stability (GEARS leads). The asymmetric recovery pattern is stable under target resampling. **d,** Endpoint hierarchy sign-consistency tile. Compact audit summary reporting the four tested contexts (HCC38, HCC1143, K562 7d, K562 13d) with the signed Δρ (CRISPR DepMap − RNAi DEMETER2). All four contexts show positive deltas (+0.215 to +0.449), confirming that CRISPR DepMap exceeds RNAi as bridge endpoint without exception. N = 4 contexts, descriptive sign consistency only; not a formal statistical test.
